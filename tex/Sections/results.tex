\chapter{Results}
\label{results}
%--------------------------------------------------------------

In this chapter we present and discuss the results obtained by the previously described models on the dataset IBM AMLSim small \cite{AMLWorld}. We will first present the results of model selection (Sections \ref{modelselres}, \ref{archres}), to then procede by analysing the results of the GAGNN models (Section \ref{res}) and comparing them to the baseline models to evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed method.

The code used for the training and evaluation of the models is available at \url{https://github.com/MartinoPapa/tesi-magistrale}.

\section{Hyperparameter Selection Results}\label{modelselres}
As described in Section~\ref{sec:loss_hp}, the model selection process is splitted into two phases to mitigate the computational cost of the grid search. As we will see, this will come at the cost of a sub-optimal model but due to the limitiations of our hardware this is still the best option.

\subsection{Loss Function Hyperparameters}
We began by performing the grid search on the loss function parameters for both binary and multiclass tasks using the GAGNN with GAT backbone. Table~\ref{tab:res_phase1_binary} and Table~\ref{tab:res_phase1_multi} show the optimal hyperparameters found by this process by optimizing the macro F1-score on the validation set.

As we can see in both binary and multiclass tasks the weight associated with the edge loss \(c_3\) is the highest value. Making the edge loss the more significant to the model performance. Especially in the multiclass task, this is expected since the edge loss is the one that predicts the actual target variable, meaning the patterns, whilst the node loss and the group loss are only based on binary classification.

Another intresting observation is that, in the binary task, the weight associated with the node loss \(c_2\) is lower than the one for the group loss \(c_1\). This suggests that the idea of trying to force the model to predict money laundering at a community level might be a good one. The aggregation into communities allows the model to indirectly expand his receptive field without adding more layers to the GNN architecture, this should mitigate the cances of incourring into oversmoothing.

In the multiclass this is no longer true, my hipothesis is that while the community prediction helps us detecting whether a community is involved in money laundering or not, it does not help us to detect the exact patterns; onece the fraudolent nodes are merged into communities we can no longer understand what type of pattern generated that community and therefore the model loses the ability to predict it.

Finally, note how in the binary task the best parameters for \(c_2\), \(c_3\) and \(w\) lay at the border of the hyperparameter search space suggesting that future works should further investigate these values.

\subsection{Architectural Hyperparameters}\label{archres}
In the second phase of model selection, we runned 4 separate grid searches for the GAGNN-GAT (Binary), GAGNN-GAT (Multiclass), GAGNN-GIN (Binary) and GAGNN-GIN (Multiclass) architectures to find the optimal hyperparameters for each of them. The results are displayed in Table \ref{tab:res_phase2_gagnn_gat_bin}, \ref{tab:res_phase2_gagnn_gat_multi}, \ref{tab:res_phase2_gagnn_gin_bin} and \ref{tab:res_phase2_gagnn_gin_multi} respectively.

The first thing to notice is that, in case of GAGNN-GAT (Binary), which is the same task tackled by the paper \cite{mainpaper}, although on a different dataset, we found different optimal parameters. In particular they mention as best parameters \texttt{hidden\_dim} = 64 and \texttt{nn\_t\_hidden\_dim} = 128, which differ from our best parameters \texttt{hidden\_dim} = 128 and \texttt{nn\_t\_hidden\_dim} = 256. 
The number of layers $L$ is the same as the one found in \cite{mainpaper}.

For the number of attention heads and the value of \(\beta\) we trusted the paper and used the same hyperparameters due to the high computational cost that would have come with their tuning. That being said, the differences in \texttt{hidden\_dim} and \texttt{nn\_t\_hidden\_dim} suggests that tuning all the architectural hyperparameters could lead to big imporvements in the model performance.

Another intresting observation is that for both GAGNN-GIN and GAGNN-GAT the optimal parameters for the multiclass task suggest the need of a bigger model compared to the binary task. This is expected since usually more complex tasks require more complex models. 
As we will see Section \ref{res}, those bigger models led to overfitting suggesting, in case of multiclass classification, the need of a bigger dataset to successfully compleate the task. Due to our hardware limitations we couldn't afford to do that, but future works should consider using the IBM AMLSim Large Dataset to overcome this issue.

Finally we notice how in GAGNN-GIN (Multiclass) the optimal number of layers is 3 which is different from the 2 of the binary case. This results toghether with the others displayed in table \ref{tab:res_phase2_gagnn_gin_multi} are to be considered with caution; as we will see GAGNN-GIN for the multiclass task didn't converge during the training and therefore the results are not reliable.

\section{Models Results}\label{res}

In this section we present and discuss the results obtained by training GAGNN with GAT and GIN backbones on both binary and multiclass classification tasks and we compare them with the results obtained by training standard GNNs on the same tasks.

As expected the binary task was easier to solve. Due to our hardware limitations we cannot afford to use a larger dataset that would allow the training of bigger models and with that better performances on the multiclass tak. The question that we will try to answer is whether the GAGNN architecture could be usefull also for multiclass detection or if it only performs well on the binary task. 

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}\label{subsec:eval_metrics}
 Let \(TP\) be the number of True Positives, \(TN\) the number of True Negatives, \(FP\) the number of False Positives, and \(FN\) the number of False Negatives.
In regard to evalutate the performance of our models, we will leverage the following classification metrics.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy}: The ratio of correctly predicted observations to the total observations.
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Precision}: The ratio of correctly predicted positive observations to the total predicted positive observations.
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Recall}: The ratio of correctly predicted positive observations to all observations in actual class.
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{F1-Score}: The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, which provides a balance between the two.
    \begin{equation}
        \text{F1-Score} = 2 \times \frac{\text{Precision} \times \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{ROC-AUC}: The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve. The ROC curve plots the True Positive Rate (\(\text{TPR} = \text{Recall}\)) against the False Positive Rate (\(\text{FPR} = \frac{FP}{FP + TN}\)) across all possible classification thresholds. While TPR measures the proportion of actual illicit transactions correctly identified, FPR measures the proportion of legitimate transactions incorrectly flagged as illicit. The AUC (Area Under the Curve) provides an aggregate measure of performance independent of any specific threshold, representing the probability that the model ranks a randomly chosen positive example higher than a randomly chosen negative one. A value of \(0.5\) indicates a model that performs no better than random guessing, while a value of \(1.0\) represents a perfect classifier. The ROC-AUC is especially informative in tasks with highly imbalanced classes, such as Anti-Money Laundering, where standard accuracy can be misleading due to the overwhelming majority of legitimate transactions.
\end{itemize}
In the multiclass setting, these metrics are computed using a macro or weighted average by treating each class as a separate binary classification problem.

\subsection{General considerations}
We begin by providing some considerations regarding training dynamics that are common to all the models.

As we can see in Figures \ref{fig:loss_gagnn_gat_bin}, \ref{fig:loss_gagnn_gat_multi}, \ref{fig:loss_gagnn_gin_bin}, \ref{fig:loss_gagnn_gin_multi}, \ref{fig:loss_baseline_gat_bin}, \ref{fig:loss_baseline_gat_multi}, \ref{fig:loss_baseline_gin_bin} and \ref{fig:loss_baseline_gin_multi}, the training loss and the patience loss, albeit less for the latter, tend to be a bit noisy. This is mainly due to the fact that the losses are computed on mini batches of 256 nodes instead of on the entire dataset. Furthermore, while is true that for the validation loss we consider all the neighborhood of the sampled nodes (\texttt{num\_neighbors} = [-1,-1,-1]); for the training mini-batches, in regard to reduce the computational cost, we only considered 15 of the adjacent neighbors, 10 neighbors for each of those 15 and, in case of a 3 layer GNN, another 10 neighbors for each of those 10 \(\times\) 15 (\texttt{num\_neighbors} = [15, 10, 10]). This introduces further noise in the loss computation and it's probably the reason why in some experiments the training loss is not as smooth as we expect it to be. 

Another intresting thing is how the GIN models tend to converge faster than the GAT ones; this is probably due to the five heads (\(H=5\)) used in the GAT model which introduces more parameters to train and with that a larger search space for the optimization algorithm to explore. Probably for the same reasons, baseline models converge faster than GAGNN models, which again introduce more parameters to train. 

An important thing to mention about the graph plots is that they show the training loss and the patience loss only up to the point in which the early stopping policy was triggered. This is clear when we look at the training history for the GAGNN-GIN Multiclass task (Figure \ref{fig:loss_gagnn_gin_multi}), where the model fails to converge and the best value reached in the 12th epoch and doesn't improve in the following 150 epochs. 

Finally, it is important to reflect on the theoretical strengths of the different architectures employed and how they map to specific money laundering patterns. Group-aware models like GAGNN are explicitly designed to capture organized, gang-level behaviors. Theoretically, they should excel at identifying structures as fraudulent (though not necessarily telling them apart) such as gather-scatter, fan-in, fan-out, bipartite, and stack patterns. This is because it should be fairly easy for the model to merge the fraudulent edges in those structures into a single community node. It is important to clarify that while this merging mechanism is extremely beneficial in the binary classification task for distinguishing illicit clusters from normal traffic, it may actually hinder performance in the multiclass task: grouping nodes into supernodes might help detect that a transaction is fraudulent, but it obfuscates the specific topological structure needed to accurately classify the exact pattern. Nonetheless, the multiclass results partially support this theoretical advantage, as GAGNN-GAT proved competitive on the gather-scatter pattern. However, this good performance might also depend on the fact that gather-scatter is the best-represented class in the dataset, with 716 labeled edges (see Figure \ref{fig:patterns_distr}).

For random and cycle patterns, the situation is different. Since these patterns can involve an arbitrary number of nodes, and our GNNs are restricted to 2 or 3 layers, the limited receptive field makes it difficult to group all of the involved nodes into a single community. Nevertheless, GAGNN should still improve the binary detection of those patterns compared to a standard GNN by allowing different nodes to merge, thus indirectly expanding the receptive field of the nodes. In our experiments, however, the model struggled significantly with the cycle class. This is likely due to the extreme scarcity of this pattern, as there are only 287 edges labeled as cycle in the entire dataset (Figure \ref{fig:patterns_distr}), which prevented the complex group-aggregation layers from learning the pattern without overfitting.

Furthermore, we must mention that the bipartite class is not correctly represented in the IBM AMLSim dataset, as it is not implemented as described in the original AMLWorld paper. Comparing the theoretical representation of the bipartite class shown in Figure \ref{fig:launderingPatterns} with the actual dataset implementation provided in Figure \ref{fig:patternsIBM}, it is clear that the topological structure differs significantly. This discrepancy is likely the main reason why the bipartite class is not well recognized in the multiclass task and could also lead to a lower performance in the binary classification task.

Regarding the backbone architectures, the core strength of GAT lies in its ability to compute hidden representations by attending over neighbors with different, learnable weights. This makes it highly effective for hub-and-spoke structures such as fan-in and fan-out patterns. The attention mechanism allows the model to assign disproportionately high weights to the anomalous edges connecting to the central hub account, effectively filtering out the noise from legitimate transactions. Lastly, GIN is theoretically proven to be as discriminative as the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph isomorphism test, making it exceptionally powerful at distinguishing exact structural graph topologies. Since it relies on sum-aggregation to capture the exact multiset of neighborhood features, GIN is best suited for rigid, structurally distinct patterns like stack or specific chained topologies, where the exact structural signature is the defining characteristic of the laundering behavior.

\subsection{Binary Classification task}

When analyzing the results for the binary classification task, it is crucial to establish the priority of the metrics involved. In the context of Anti-Money Laundering (AML), the primary objective is to detect as many illicit transactions as possible. Therefore, achieving a high Recall on the fraudulent class (labeled as \textit{Laundering}) is far more important than maximizing overall accuracy or precision. Failing to flag a money laundering attempt as suspicious (a False Negative) could lead to severe legal and financial repercussions for a financial institution, which vastly outweigh the operational cost of investigating a legitimate account that was falsely flagged (a False Positive).

Bearing this in mind, the GAGNN-GIN model yielded the most compelling results from a strict risk-aversion standpoint. It achieved an outstanding recall of \(0.86\) on the Laundering class, significantly outperforming the standard GIN baseline (\(0.71\)). However, this high sensitivity came at the cost of precision (\(0.54\)), leading to a higher number of false positives which dragged its overall accuracy down to \(0.8778\). 

If a more balanced approach is desired, the GAGNN-GAT model proved to be the best overall performer. It achieved the highest Accuracy (\(0.9268\)), ROC-AUC (\(0.9065\)), and F1-Score (\(0.75\)) on the minority class among all the tested models. Crucially, it maintained a strong recall of \(0.76\) while keeping precision relatively high (\(0.73\)), proving that the GAGNN architecture successfully improved upon the standard GAT baseline across every single metric.

\subsection{Multiclass Classification task}

Unlike the binary setting, the multiclass classification task proved to be exceedingly difficult for the GAGNN architecture. The best performance was surprisingly achieved by the standard GIN baseline model, which reached an accuracy of \(0.4526\) and a ROC-AUC of \(0.7408\). In contrast, the GAGNN models struggled significantly: the GAGNN-GAT achieved an accuracy of only \(0.2810\), while the GAGNN-GIN model effectively failed to learn, halting its convergence early with an accuracy of \(0.1187\). 

There are several factors that could explain why the GAGNN models failed in this scenario.

First and foremost is the fact that the GNN, whether it's a GAT or a GIN, shares the same weights for both group level and node level prediction. Since after the grouping stage nodes are pooled and aggregated together, the task of predicting what pattern generated that group becomes very difficult for the GNN layers. While is true that \(\mathcal{L}_{groups}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{nodes}\) both are BCE even in the multiclass task, there is a high probability that the group level loss activly prevents the model from learning the right patterns. Also in this case future work regarding a wider exploration of the Hypothesis Space \(\mathcal{H}\) by trying more values for \(c_1\) could better explain this phenomenon.

The second major factor is the extreme data imbalance combined with the small size of the dataset. While the binary task simply grouped all anomalous topologies into a single \textit{Laundering} class, the multiclass task splits them into eight distinct laundering patterns (e.g., \textit{CYCLE}, \textit{FAN-IN}, \textit{BIPARTITE}, \dots).
Since the GAGNN architectures introduce a significantly higher number of trainable parameters and a much more complex optimization landscape compared to standard GNNs, when forced to learn complex decision boundaries for 9 different classes with such limited data, the model is likely to suffer from severe overfitting and often fails to generalize. The simpler baseline models, having fewer parameters, were slightly more robust to this lack of data.
This doesn't necessary mean that the GAGNN architectures are not suitable for this task but it's clear that a bigger dataset is necessary to properly leverage its architectural complexity. 

Regarding the failure to converge for the GAGNN-GIN model, this can also be blamed on the assumption that optimal values of \(c_1,c_2\) and \(c_3\) found for the GAT model can be directly translated to the GIN model. The two architectures, albeit very similar, could work better with different degrees of importance assigned to the different loss components.

\section{Limitations}
\label{Limitations}

Here we resume the limitations encountered during the study. Most of them are related to the poor hardware resources that we had available during this work.

The first major limitation is the fact that we didn't explored the hypothesis space of the parameters as much as we would have liked to. The assumption that $c_1,c_2,c_3$ translate directly from GAT to GIN, didn't always hold as we discovered during the experimentation. Furthermore the value of \(c_1\) was kept fixed to 0.75 for all experiments, this allowed us to highly reduce the model selection computational cost, but it is possible that, especially for the multiclass task where from a theoretical analysis we belive that the group loss should hold less importance, we could have found better configurations. On top of that for architectural hyperparameters we often found the optimal values at the edges of the explored space, this suggests that the true best settings could lie outside of it.

Another problem came with the small size of the dataset, with the binary task it worked fine but for the multiclass one the need of a bigger dataset, that would have allowed the training of bigger models without overfitting, is clearly visible in both the training and test results.
This limitation is interconnected with the first one since a bigger dataset would probably allow to train bigger models and thus we would have to further expand the hypothesis space.

Finally, limiting the number of neighbors during the training of the models to 15, 10 and 10 for the first three layers respectively (\texttt{num\_neighbors} = [15,10,10]), in order to reduce the computational cost, introduced further noise in the loss computation and it's probably the reason why in some experiments the training loss is not as smooth as we expect it to be. Furthermore the fact that a froudolent neighbor could not be sampled amongst the 15 ones the detection of the fraudulent community could be seriously compromised.
