\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{sec:concl}

This dissertation investigated the effectiveness of Graph Neural Network based models for the detection of money laundering in financial transaction networks, addressing both binary and multiclass classification settings on the IBM AMLSim HI-Small synthetic dataset. The study evaluated the Group-Aware Graph Neural Network (GAGNN) architecture proposed in~\cite{mainpaper} using two different backbones, a Graph Attention Network (GAT) and a Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN), and compared them against the corresponding standard GNN baselines. Three specific research inquiries were formulated in the Introduction (\ref{intro}) and are addressed below.

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Does the GAGNN architecture outperform the baseline models on the binary task?}
    The answer is affirmative. Both GAGNN variants demonstrated clear improvements over their respective baselines on the binary classification task. The GAGNN-GAT achieved the best overall balance of performance, reaching an accuracy of \(0.9268\), a ROC-AUC of \(0.9065\), and an F1-Score of \(0.75\) on the minority class, improving over the standard GAT baseline on every metric (see Figures~\ref{fig:eval_gagnn_gat_bin} and~\ref{fig:eval_baseline_gat_bin}). The GAGNN-GIN, while yielding lower overall accuracy (\(0.8778\)), achieved an outstanding recall of \(0.86\) on the Laundering class, substantially outperforming the standard GIN baseline (\(0.71\)) and making it the preferred model in high-risk, recall-oriented deployment scenarios (see Figures~\ref{fig:eval_gagnn_gin_bin} and~\ref{fig:eval_baseline_gin_bin}). These results confirm that the community-aware grouping mechanism introduced by GAGNN adds genuine discriminative value for binary fraud detection, effectively acting as an implicit receptive field expansion without requiring additional GNN layers.

    \item \textbf{Could the GIN backbone outperform the GAT backbone within the GAGNN framework?}
    Empirically, the GIN backbone consistently underperformed relative to the GAT one when used as the backbone for GAGNN, both in terms of F1-Score on the binary task and convergence stability on the multiclass task. Several hypotheses can be advanced to explain this gap. First, the multi-head attention mechanism of GAT~(Section~\ref{sec:gat}) allows the model to simultaneously attend to both node-level and group-level dependencies through different heads, which may be inherently better suited to the dual-objective nature of the GAGNN loss. GIN, by contrast, relies on a single aggregation path and may struggle to separate the two learning signals. Second, the theoretical expressiveness of GIN, namely its ability to match the 1-WL test~(Section~\ref{sec:mp_limits}), may be partially undermined by the grouping phase: once nodes are merged into supernodes, the original structural signatures that GIN is designed to discriminate are no longer preserved, making its theoretical advantage largely irrelevant in this context. Finally, as explicitly discussed in Section~\ref{Limitations}, the assumption that the optimal loss hyperparameters \(c_1, c_2, c_3\) and \(w\) found for the GAT backbone can be directly transferred to the GIN one did not always hold. This is particularly evident in the multiclass setting, where GAGNN-GIN failed to converge entirely (Figure~\ref{fig:loss_gagnn_gin_multi}), suggesting that a dedicated hyperparameter search for the GIN variant could substantially change this conclusion.

    \item \textbf{Can the GAGNN architecture be extended to the multiclass pattern classification task?}
    In its current configuration and on the available dataset, the GAGNN architecture did not perform well on the multiclass task. Both the GAGNN-GAT (\(\text{accuracy} = 0.2810\), \(\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.6639\)) and the GAGNN-GIN, which didn't converge, were significantly outperformed by the standard GIN baseline (\(\text{accuracy} = 0.4526\), \(\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.7408\)), see Figures~\ref{fig:eval_gagnn_gat_multi},~\ref{fig:eval_gagnn_gin_multi} and~\ref{fig:eval_baseline_gin_multi}. 
    
    The most plausible explanation is that the very mechanism that makes GAGNN effective for binary detection, that is the merging of suspicious nodes into community supernodes, actively destroys the fine-grained topological structure that distinguishes one laundering pattern from another. Once fraudulent edges are pooled together, the specific signature of a fan-in, a scatter-gather, or a cycle is irrecoverably lost, and the GNN layers are forced to predict the pattern type from an already-obfuscated representation. This is further supported by the observation that, as noted in Section~\ref{Limitations}, the group loss weight \(c_1\) was held fixed at \(0.75\) throughout all multiclass experiments. Trying lower values of \(c_1\), assigning less importance to the group loss, might allow the node-level and edge-level components of the loss to preserve more structural information.
    
    Additionally, the size of the HI-Small dataset~(Section~\ref{sec:datdesc}) also contributed to the poor results in the multiclass task; with so few labeled examples per pattern class, overfitting was observed during the training phase. This prevented the models from learning the actual patterns and from exploring an hypothesis space consisting of more complex models.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Directions}
%--------------------------------------------------------------
\label{futureDirections}

Several promising directions emerge from the limitations and findings of this study.

\textbf{Scaling up the experimental setup.}
The most immediate direction for future work is to extend the experimental evaluation to larger datasets. As discussed in Section~\ref{Limitations}, the IBM AMLSim HI-Small dataset proved insufficient for the multiclass task, preventing the training of larger models without incurring severe overfitting. Using the IBM AMLSim Large variant would provide substantially more labeled examples per pattern class, allowing the exploration of a wider hypothesis space and potentially unlocking the full representational capacity of the GAGNN architecture. Alongside a larger dataset, a more thorough grid search over the loss function hyperparameters should be conducted. In particular, the group loss weight \(c_1\) was held fixed at \(0.75\) throughout all experiments; a dedicated search over this parameter, especially for the multiclass setting where community aggregation is theoretically less beneficial, could reveal significantly better configurations. Furthermore, the assumption that the optimal values of \(c_1, c_2, c_3\) and \(w\) found for the GAT backbone directly translate to the GIN backbone should be abandoned: the two architectures have fundamentally different aggregation mechanisms and may require independent tuning of the loss weights to reach their respective performance peaks.

\textbf{Pattern-aware adaptations of the GAGNN components.}
A more principled direction is to redesign the GAGNN components to be natively aware of laundering pattern types rather than operating purely in the binary licit/illicit space. In the current formulation, the eMRF layer encourages nodes that are involved in the same suspected fraud ring to produce similar embeddings, but the notion of similarity is defined only with respect to the binary label; two nodes belonging to different pattern types (e.g., a fan-in and a cycle) are pushed together as long as both are illicit. A natural extension would be to condition the eMRF smoothing on the pattern label, so that only nodes involved in the same structural pattern type attract each other in the embedding space. Similarly, the node-level loss \(\mathcal{L}_{nodes}\) and the group-level loss \(\mathcal{L}_{groups}\) could be reformulated as multiclass objectives, where the target label for each node or community is derived from the majority pattern type of its associated transactions, encoded as a one-hot vector and averaged across the neighborhood. This would allow the group loss to actively guide the model toward learning pattern-discriminative community representations rather than coarse binary ones, potentially recovering much of the structural information currently lost in the pooling phase.

\textbf{Two-stage pipeline for explainable pattern detection.}
A complementary approach is to decouple the binary and multiclass tasks by applying them sequentially in a two-stage pipeline. In the first stage, a binary model, such as the GAGNN-GAT which showed the best overall performance, would be used to flag each transaction as licit or illicit. In the second stage, a separate pattern classifier would be applied exclusively to the edges identified as illicit in the first stage, reducing the problem to a classification over a much smaller and more balanced subset of transactions. This formulation has several advantages: it simplifies the optimization landscape of the second stage by removing the overwhelming majority of licit transactions, it allows the pattern classifier to be trained and tuned independently without the interference of the binary community loss, and it introduces a natural degree of explainability, since every flagged transaction would be accompanied not only by a binary alert but also by a predicted laundering pattern that human analysts can directly inspect and interpret.
